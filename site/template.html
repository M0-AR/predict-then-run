The problem

Most experiments are stories told after the result.

When the answer is already on the screen, it is easy to sound wise. That is a problem for research, for product teams, and for anyone buying a technical claim. The fix is not more confidence. It is a better order of operations.

Hindsight bias

A result always looks obvious after you see it. Without a pre-committed prediction, the story can bend to fit the data.

Hidden failures

Failed experiments rarely become blog posts. The result is a literature and a market that look more certain than they are.

Unreproducible demos

A chart is not an audit trail. If the seed, the claim, and the scoring rule are not fixed, the demo cannot be checked.

The inversion: write the claim first. Give it a direction, a confidence, and a falsifier. Freeze it. Only then run the experiment. The result can confirm the claim, refute it, or leave it unresolved — and every outcome is publishable.

The method

Four steps. No post-hoc storytelling.

The protocol is deliberately small enough to inspect. It borrows the discipline of pre-registration and turns it into a claim-level scoring system for machine-learning experiments.

Claim

Write a sentence that can be wrong. Add a direction, a predicted size, a confidence, and a falsifier.

Freeze

Hash the payload and chain it to the previous entry. Editing the claim after the fact breaks the chain.

Run

Run a controlled experiment with fixed seeds and a matched budget. Small enough to repeat; real enough to surprise you.

Score

Use paired bootstrap intervals, Brier scores, calibration, and a verdict rule fixed before the result.

Three results worth your attention.

One useful confirmation, one clean refutation, and one honest maybe. The refutation is not a footnote. It is the product working as intended.

The gate recovers updated-fact accuracy under a stale memory. But it does so by suppressing hints: OLD-fact top-1 falls from 100% to 8.3%. That is a trade-off, not a free lunch.

From zero to confident reader

Learn the method in 15 minutes.

No statistics background required. Start with the plain-language path, then take the quiz. Every answer is explained, and the score is only a teaching device — not a certification.

1

Start with the sentence

A claim is a sentence that can be wrong. “The new onboarding will raise activation” is not a claim until you add a direction, a size, and a time window.

2

Freeze it

Write the claim, the predicted direction, the confidence, and the falsifier. Hashing makes later edits visible. That is the difference between a prediction and a memory.

3

Read the verdict

Confirmed means the interval excludes zero and the sign matches. Refuted means the interval excludes zero and the sign is opposite. Inconclusive means the interval includes zero.

4

Ask what it does not prove

A confirmed tiny-scale result is not a law of nature. It is evidence about one regime. The limitations section is part of the result, not an apology.

5

Use it on a real decision

Pick the claim that would change what you do. If a wrong answer is cheap, test it. If it is expensive, pre-register it before you look.

Small enough to run. Complete enough to audit.
